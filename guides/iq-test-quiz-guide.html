IQ Test Quiz: Questions and How to Improve

What IQ Tests Actually Measure

An IQ test measures reasoning ability — how quickly and accurately you can spot patterns, solve unfamiliar problems and think logically. It does not measure your knowledge, your memory of facts, or your worth as a person. Most tests include number series, shape patterns, analogies, odd-one-out questions, spatial reasoning and short logic problems. Employers, the armed forces and some universities use IQ-style tests in selection, and the analytical section of NTS and similar exams is built on the same skills. Your score can improve with practice, because familiarity with the question types removes the surprise factor.

The Question Types You Will Meet

Number series ask for the next number in a sequence: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30 follows the pattern of adding 4, 6, 8, 10, so the next is 42. Analogies ask for a relationship: 'Book is to reading as pen is to writing.' Odd-one-out questions give four items and ask which does not belong. Shape patterns ask which figure completes a sequence or a matrix. Coding questions replace letters with numbers or symbols by a rule you must discover. Blood relation questions test careful reading: 'A is B's brother, B is C's mother' and so on. Each type has a small set of patterns; learning them is the fastest way to raise your score.

How to Improve Your Score

Practise a little every day rather than in one long session. Time yourself, because IQ tests are always against the clock. Learn the standard patterns for number series: differences, multiplication, squares and cubes, and alternating sequences. For shape questions, look for what changes between figures — rotation, number of sides, shading, or the addition and removal of elements. Do puzzles like Sudoku and non-verbal reasoning workbooks. Sleep well before a test: tired brains spot patterns far more slowly. And read each question twice — many IQ questions are missed not because they are hard, but because the question was misread.

What IQ Scores Mean — and What They Don't

IQ scores are usually set so that 100 is the average, with most people scoring between 85 and 115. A score above 130 is generally described as very high, and below 70 as very low, but a single number never captures a person's abilities. Creativity, emotional intelligence, practical skills and hard work are not measured by IQ tests at all. Treat an IQ-style test as a game with rules you can learn, prepare honestly, and do not pay for apps or websites that promise to raise your IQ by a fixed number — no honest source can guarantee that. For official selection tests, check the recruiting organisation's own guidance.

Ten Practice Questions

Try these now, without rushing. What comes next: 5, 10, 20, 40? (80 — doubling.) Which is the odd one out: apple, banana, carrot, mango? (Carrot — it is a vegetable.) Book is to reading as pen is to? (Writing.) What comes next: 3, 6, 11, 18? (27 — differences of 3, 5, 7, then 9.) If all Bloops are Razzies and all Razzies are Lazzies, are all Bloops Lazzies? (Yes.) How many triangles are in a square cut by one diagonal? (Two.) Which number is missing: 2, 4, 8, 16, __? (32.) A is taller than B, B is taller than C. Who is shortest? (C.) What is half of a quarter of 100? (12.5.) Which comes next: A, C, F, J? (O — gaps of 1, 2, 3, then 4 letters.) Score yourself out of ten and repeat similar puzzles weekly; pattern-spotting improves fastest with short, frequent practice under a timer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I improve my IQ test score?
A: Yes — practising the common question types and working under time pressure usually raises scores, because familiarity removes the surprise factor.

Q: What is the average IQ score?
A: 100 is set as the average on most IQ scales, with most people scoring between 85 and 115.

Q: Do IQ tests measure knowledge?
A: No. They measure reasoning and pattern-spotting, not facts you have memorised.
