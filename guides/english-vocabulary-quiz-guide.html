English Vocabulary Quiz: Words and Practice

Why Vocabulary Decides English Scores

In CSS, PPSC and NTS English papers, vocabulary questions — synonyms, antonyms, idioms and one-word substitutions — carry a large share of the marks. Grammar can be learned from rules, but vocabulary only grows with exposure. The trap is trying to memorise long word lists without context: words learned without a sentence are forgotten within days. The method that works is learning a few words deeply every day — meaning, one synonym, one antonym and one example sentence — and then meeting them again in reading.

Words That Appear Again and Again

Certain words repeat in Pakistani exam papers year after year. 'Abundant' means plentiful; its opposite is 'scarce'. 'Benevolent' means kind and generous; its opposite is 'malevolent'. 'Candid' means frank and honest. 'Diligent' means hardworking. 'Frugal' means careful with money. 'Hostile' means unfriendly. 'Obsolete' means no longer in use. 'Transparent' means clear or easy to see through. Learn these high-frequency words first, then add idioms: 'to break the ice' means to start a conversation, 'a blessing in disguise' means something good that seemed bad at first, and 'to hit the nail on the head' means to say exactly the right thing.

How to Learn Words So They Stick

Use each new word in a sentence of your own the same day you learn it — writing beats reading for memory. Group words by theme: words about character (honest, brave, humble), words about size (huge, tiny, vast) and words about feelings (joyful, anxious, calm). Read one English newspaper editorial a day and note five new words; newspapers are the best source because exam vocabulary comes from the same register. Review old words every Sunday using flashcards, and test yourself with the quiz tools on this site.

Common Mistakes in Vocabulary Quizzes

The most common mistake is choosing a word that sounds right but means something slightly different — for example, confusing 'elicit' (to draw out) with 'illicit' (unlawful). Another is ignoring the exact shade of meaning: 'happy' and 'ecstatic' are synonyms, but only one fits a sentence about mild pleasure. In antonym questions, watch for words with prefixes like 'un-', 'in-' and 'dis-', which often signal the opposite. Finally, never guess an idiom from its literal words; idioms must be learned as whole phrases, because 'raining cats and dogs' has nothing to do with animals.

Words to Learn This Week

Here are ten high-frequency exam words to learn this week, each with a synonym and a sentence. 'Abundant' (plentiful): 'The region has abundant water.' 'Benevolent' (kind): 'The benevolent teacher helped every student.' 'Candid' (frank): 'She gave a candid opinion.' 'Diligent' (hardworking): 'Diligent students revise daily.' 'Frugal' (careful with money): 'He lived a frugal life.' 'Hostile' (unfriendly): 'The hostile crowd shouted loudly.' 'Obsolete' (outdated): 'Typewriters are now obsolete.' 'Transparent' (clear): 'The glass is transparent.' 'Vigilant' (watchful): 'Guards must stay vigilant.' 'Zealous' (enthusiastic): 'The zealous volunteer worked all day.' Write each word on a flashcard with its meaning and sentence, review them each morning, and use two of them in your own sentences every day. By Sunday, test yourself: cover the meanings and recall them. Next week, add ten more.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the synonym of 'abundant'?
A: 'Plentiful'. Its antonym is 'scarce'.

Q: How many new words should I learn per day?
A: Five words a day, each with a sentence of your own, beats fifty words skimmed from a list.

Q: Do idioms appear in CSS and PPSC papers?
A: Yes, idioms and phrasal verbs are a regular part of English papers in Pakistani competitive exams.
