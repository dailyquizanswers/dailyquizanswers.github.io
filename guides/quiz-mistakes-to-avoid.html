Quiz Mistakes to Avoid (and How to Fix Them)

Mistake 1: Trusting Memory Without Checking

The most common quiz mistake is answering from a half-remembered fact and never checking it. Many people 'know' that the capital of Australia is Sydney (it is Canberra), or that the Great Wall of China is visible from space (it is not). The fix is simple: when you are only 60% sure of an answer, say so to yourself and check it afterwards. Keep a notebook of facts you were unsure about and verify each one from a reliable source. Over time, your 'unsure' list shrinks, and your accuracy climbs. In exams, this habit also stops you from writing a confident wrong answer.

Mistake 2: Not Reading the Question Properly

A large share of lost marks comes from misreading the question. 'Which of the following is NOT true?' is answered as if it asked what IS true. 'First' is read as 'last'. 'Largest' is read as 'longest'. The fix is to slow down for five seconds on every question: underline the key word — not, first, largest, except — before looking at the options. In timed tests, this feels slow, but it saves more marks than it costs. Practise with the timer tool on this site and make 'read the question twice' a habit before the exam, not during it.

Mistake 3: Cramming and Skipping Revision

Cramming feels productive and fails reliably. Facts learned in one long session fade within days, while facts revised four times over a month stay for years. The fix is a revision cycle: learn a topic, revise it tomorrow, again in three days, again in a week, and again in a month. Use the Score Tracker to see whether your scores are actually improving — if they are flat, your method needs changing, not more hours. Sleep is part of revision too: memory is consolidated during sleep, so a good night before a test beats an all-nighter.

Mistake 4: Guessing Blindly and Ignoring the Clock

Blind guessing is a poor strategy even when there is no negative marking: a random guess has a one-in-four chance, but eliminating two wrong options first raises it to one in two. Always eliminate what you know is wrong before guessing. The second half of this mistake is time: spending five minutes on one hard question while ten easy ones go unanswered. The fix is a simple rule — if a question takes more than a minute, mark it and move on, and return to it at the end. In the last five minutes, answer everything remaining, because an unanswered question scores zero for certain.

Your Personal Error Log

Start an error log today with four columns: date, question, correct answer, and lesson. After every practice session, add your mistakes. Within two weeks a pattern will appear — most learners find that 80% of their errors come from just two or three causes: misreading questions, mixing up similar facts (like the first constitution of 1956 and the current one of 1973), or weak topics like everyday science. Fix the cause, not the individual question: if misreading is your problem, practise underlining key words; if similar facts confuse you, make a comparison table; if a topic is weak, give it a focused week. Review the log every Sunday and watch the pattern shrink. An error log turns every mistake into a lesson, which is the fastest way to improve a quiz score.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the biggest mistake in quiz exams?
A: Misreading the question — especially words like 'not', 'first' and 'except'. Read every question twice.

Q: Should I guess if I don't know the answer?
A: If there is no negative marking, guess — but first eliminate the options you know are wrong to improve your odds.

Q: Does cramming work for quizzes?
A: No. Short, repeated revision over days and weeks works far better than one long session.
