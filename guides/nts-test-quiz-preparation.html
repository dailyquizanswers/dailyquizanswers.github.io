NTS Test Quiz Preparation Guide

What NTS Tests Look Like

The National Testing Service (NTS) conducts recruitment and admission tests across Pakistan, including the NAT for university admissions and GAT for graduate admissions. Most NTS papers follow a familiar pattern: a verbal (English) section, an analytical reasoning section and a quantitative (mathematics) section, with subject knowledge added for job tests. The questions are multiple-choice, the time is tight, and there is usually no negative marking — though you should always confirm the marking scheme on your test's official instructions. Because the pattern repeats, preparation is mostly about speed and familiarity.

The Three Core Sections

The verbal section tests vocabulary (synonyms and antonyms), analogies, sentence completion and reading comprehension. The analytical section tests logical reasoning: sequence and series, odd-one-out, coding-decoding, blood relations and logical deductions. The quantitative section tests arithmetic, percentages, averages, ratio and proportion, algebra and basic geometry. Each section rewards a different skill, so prepare them separately: vocabulary cards for verbal, puzzle practice for analytical, and timed drills for quantitative. The maths and vocabulary guides on this site cover the first two in detail.

A Four-Week Practice Plan

In week one, take a full past paper under timed conditions to find your weakest section. In weeks two and three, drill that weak section daily — thirty minutes of focused practice — while maintaining the strong sections with ten questions a day. In week four, take a full timed paper every other day and review every mistake the same evening. Learn to skip hard questions and return to them: on a no-negative-marking paper, every question should eventually get an answer, but your time should go first to the questions you can solve quickly.

Test-Day Strategy

Reach the centre early with your roll number slip and original CNIC, as the test instructions require. Read the instructions on the paper before starting, and confirm whether there is negative marking. Answer the section you are strongest in first to build confidence, and keep an eye on the clock — aim to finish with ten minutes spare for review. Do not spend more than a minute on any single question in the first pass; mark it and move on. In the last five minutes, fill in any unanswered questions rather than leaving them blank. Always check the official NTS website for your test date, centre and result — never trust forwarded messages.

Analytical Reasoning: Worked Example

Analytical questions look hard until you learn the patterns. Example: 'In a code, CAT is written as 3120. How is DOG written?' Each letter is replaced by its position in the alphabet: C is 3, A is 1, T is 20, so DOG is D=4, O=15, G=7, giving 4157. Another common type is the series: 2, 5, 10, 17, 26 — the differences are 3, 5, 7, 9, so the next difference is 11 and the next number is 37. Blood relations: 'A is the brother of B. B is the mother of C.' So A is the maternal uncle of C. Practise ten questions of each type — coding, series, odd-one-out and relations — and the patterns become automatic. In the test, draw small diagrams for relation questions; a quick sketch prevents the careless errors that cost marks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What does NTS stand for?
A: National Testing Service, Pakistan's main testing organisation for admissions and recruitment tests.

Q: Is there negative marking in NTS tests?
A: Most NTS tests have no negative marking, but always confirm the marking scheme in your own test's official instructions.

Q: How should I prepare for the NAT?
A: Practise verbal, analytical and quantitative questions daily, and take full timed past papers in the final two weeks.
